# Narration Practices for Long Gameplay

Use this contract when deciding whether a long gameplay project needs proposed post-recorded narration. Narration is optional. It must solve a continuity or understanding problem that retained source audio and footage cannot solve cleanly.

## Establish the production contract

Before proposing narration, classify the project and opening:

- `discovery_first_look`: preserve changing knowledge, surprise, mistakes, and later understanding.
- `constraint_challenge`: make the constraint, attempt state, adaptation, compliance, and outcome legible.
- `gameplay_continuous`: preserve execution, attrition, improvisation, or attempt integrity.
- `narration_led_retrospective`: build an honest causal account from fragmented sessions, retries, dense systems, or substantial omissions.

Also choose `narrated_setup`, `cold_open`, or `gameplay_first`. Treat that choice as a viewer-facing contract. Later narration is natural when the opening establishes an explanatory or retrospective mode. When a gameplay-first opening establishes continuous immediacy, introduce later narration only at a clearly signaled structural change.

Use the user's stated objective as evidence. A first playthrough, challenge run, sequel, familiar game, or obscure game can justify different amounts of setup. Do not infer a universal opening formula from the reference videos.

## Decide whether narration earns its place

Propose narration only when at least one of these jobs is necessary:

- Explain an opaque mechanic, choice consequence, route requirement, or later-discovered fact.
- Connect discontinuous footage across travel, retries, resets, sessions, or changed run state.
- Turn repeated attempts into a concise learning arc without inventing certainty.
- Reorient the viewer after dense lore, a major omission, or a change of objective.
- Synthesize relationships or lore needed for a later payoff.
- Establish a boss threat or strategy when the retained encounter does not communicate it alone.
- Create a factual callback between an earlier mistake or setup and a later solution or payoff.
- Mediate censored or unsuitable source material while preserving necessary context.
- Disclose guide use, outside knowledge, reconstructed material, or external footage.
- Explain the real ending, stopping point, unresolved outcome, or alternate route.

Do not narrate plainly visible actions beat by beat. Do not propose narration merely because a section is long, quiet, or difficult to summarize. Remove narration that supplies no new context, consequence, structure, emotion, or interpretation.

## Build the narration passage

For every proposed passage:

1. State the viewer knowledge gap it closes.
2. State the factual points the creator should cover as one cohesive thought, not an information dump.
3. Identify the source evidence that supports each important claim.
4. Suggest footage that demonstrates the explanation: the relevant choice, menu, item, route change, failed attempt, reaction, state change, or payoff.
5. End at the earliest live utterance or source event that can carry the reaction or payoff itself.

The normal rhythm is:

`source discovery or setup → concise retrospective explanation → source reaction or payoff`

The footage under narration must be evidence-bearing, not generic wallpaper. It may be assembled nonlinearly, but meaningful changes in time, session, knowledge, route, attempt, or provenance must remain honest and legible.

## Preserve the source's strengths

- Keep live audio for surprise, confusion, frustration, humor, decision pressure, and authentic discovery.
- Keep readable game text, menus, item descriptions, dialogue, or outcome screens when the narration relies on them.
- Keep enough combat to establish the relevant mechanic, reversal, danger, and result. Routine turns may be omitted.
- For first looks, never rewrite later knowledge into apparent first-run certainty.
- For challenge runs, never compress away attempt continuity or evidence needed to verify the constraint.
- Clearly distinguish creator footage from browser clips, guides, another creator's footage, or reconstructions.

## Avoid false signals

Polished wording is not proof that a source passage is post-recorded. Phrases such as “it turned out” or a coherent strategic recap can occur live. When studying references, classify narration at the utterance level and require corroboration from source alignment, hindsight knowledge, or deliberate explanatory underlay.

For the application, narration does not exist yet: produce a brief for the user rather than pretending to recover a finished script. One robust narration passage is preferable to several adjacent briefs covering the same continuous idea.

## Evidence behind this contract

The ConnorDawg *Fear & Hunger* study compared a 1:01:05 finished video with 13:03:40 of source VODs, a 12.83:1 gross compression ratio. The finished video retains discoveries, choices, reactions, mechanics, lore, boss evidence, and outcomes while omitting most traversal, administration, backtracking, repeated attempts, and downtime.

Transcript alignment matched 42.2% of finished speech to VOD speech. This is not a narration percentage: unmatched speech also includes caption errors, edited fragments, and external material. Manual source checks found likely post-recorded narration throughout the finished video, especially for choice consequences, mechanics, recaps, censorship context, guide-assisted strategy, and ending logic. They also rejected three plausible-looking false positives that were actually live source speech.

The strongest demonstrated narration thread is an early wasted Empty Scroll followed much later by its correct use. Narration explains both the hidden mechanic and the later payoff. Near the ending, narration bridges a roughly 200-minute source jump into alternate-ending material and explicitly discloses watched external footage.
